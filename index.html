Understand my science

What does this measurement mean?

Sap velocity tells us how quickly water travels through the water-conducting tissue (xylem) in my trunk.

Source: local sap flow sensor · point measurement

Why does this matter for sustainability?

Transpiration helps cool leaves and the surrounding air. Heat and drought can change water transport, and healthy roots help sustain this service.

Landscape lesson: protect my roots and soil so I can continue to take up water.

My canopy intercepts sunlight, and water evaporating from my leaves absorbs heat. This paired contrast illustrates a local microclimate difference; it cannot isolate transpiration from shade or other site effects.

What do temperature and humidity tell us?

Air temperature describes how warm the air is near the sensors. Relative humidity tells us how close the air is to saturation at that temperature. Measuring both at comparable sites helps describe the microclimate and calculate how strongly the air draws water from leaves.

These are example readings, not actual observations. The pilot would use matched, radiation-shielded sensors at the same height in comparable locations.

Rain interception

Model-based

My leaves and branches intercept rain, some of which evaporates before reaching the ground. A modeled benefit, using weather and canopy data.

Rainfall from a nearby weather station and canopy characteristics can support an estimate. Interception is not the same as total runoff avoided.

How are these benefits estimated?

Carbon uses tree species, diameter and height in biomass equations. Annual gain uses measured or modeled growth. Water use requires scaling sap velocity to the conducting sapwood; cooling is derived from estimated transpiration. Rain interception can be modeled with local weather and canopy information. Estimates have uncertainty and change over time.

My ecological neighbors

What wildlife might visit me?

Oaks support caterpillars and other insects, which in turn provide food for birds. Acorns are eaten and dispersed by animals such as squirrels and jays. Cavities and branches can also provide shelter.

Climate-smart care
What can we do?

Help trees thrive in a changing climate.

Protect their rooting space, avoid soil compaction, choose species suited to their site, manage water responsibly, and preserve healthy mature canopies. Together these practices can help trees keep delivering shade, cooling and other environmental benefits.

Water and protect my roots

When my soil dries out, I may reduce water use to conserve moisture. Appropriate watering and mulch can help retain soil water. Avoid compacting soil around my roots so water and air can reach them.

Choose the right tree for the right place

Trees differ in their responses to heat, drought and rooting conditions. Choosing species suited to the site and protecting established trees helps sustain shade, habitat and other benefits.
